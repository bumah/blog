For a long time I thought the person leading a conversation was the one doing most of the talking. It's almost exactly backwards. The people who quietly run a room are usually the ones asking the questions, not answering them.

Leading a conversation doesn't mean steering it toward you. It means the opposite — making the other person feel like the most interesting person in the room, and letting the exchange go somewhere useful on the way. That's a little counterintuitive, which is why most people get it wrong.

These are four habits I've realised matter for leading conversations, and for leading people. They spell LEAD: Listening, Emotions, Attention, Digging. Two of them you manage inside yourself; two of them you show toward the other person.

L — Listening

Most of us listen with a loaded response, just waiting for our turn. People feel that. The shift is to listen only to understand, and let your next line come from what they actually said — not from what you pre-loaded while they were still talking.

Listening is the input. Get this wrong and nothing else works.

E — Emotions

This is the one about you, not them. If you get defensive, impatient, or need to be right, the conversation stops being about the other person. Managing your own reactions — staying calm, not taking the bait, not jumping in to correct — is what keeps you in the driver's seat.

You can't listen well to someone if you're busy reacting to them.

A — Attention

Listening is internal. Attention is the signal back. It's what makes the other person actually feel heard, seen, and understood — reflecting what they said, acknowledging it, holding eye contact, remembering the small thing they mentioned last time.

Someone can be listening and still feel cold. Attention is the warmth that proves you were there.

D — Digging

This is what separates leading a conversation from just receiving one. Be curious. Ask the open question — "what made you get into that?" — and then follow the thread. "Say more about that." Let them talk. Whoever asks the questions quietly sets the direction.

Digging is how you take a flat exchange and find the part that actually matters to them.

The test

The simplest test of a conversation well led: does the other person walk away feeling a little more interesting, a little more understood, than before? Do that consistently and people seek you out. It costs nothing but attention.

I'm still worse at this than I'd like. But treating conversation as something you lead — with Listening, Emotions, Attention, and Digging — rather than something that happens to you, has quietly changed how most of my days go.
