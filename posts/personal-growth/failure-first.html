Most advice tells you to focus on success — picture the win, plan the path to it, stay positive. There's another way in, and it's the one I keep coming back to: don't aim at success, aim at not failing.

In probability terms, success is just one minus failure. S = 1 − F. If you drive the chance of failure down, the chance of success goes up by exactly the same amount. So instead of straining toward the win, I work on removing the ways it could go wrong.

Borrowed from two places

The first is the premortem. Before you start, you imagine it's over and the thing has already failed — then you work backwards and ask why. Flipping the timeline like this surfaces risks that stay invisible when you're only picturing success.

The second is risk management in banking, where I first properly used this. You don't run a bank by hoping things go well. You assume losses will happen, map where they'll come from, and put controls in front of the biggest ones. Same instinct, applied to anything you're trying to pull off.

The three-cause exercise

Here's the whole method. Before I commit to something, I assume I've already failed at it, then ask one question:

What are the three things most likely to have caused that failure?

Not ten. Three. Forcing it down to three keeps you honest about what actually matters instead of drowning in every possible risk. These are usually obvious once you look — the ones you already half-knew but were quietly hoping to ignore.

Then lower the odds

For each of the three, one more question: how do I make it less likely? Not eliminate it — you rarely can — just cut the odds. A small safeguard here, a bit of preparation there, a plan for when it wobbles. Each one you defuse pulls F down, which pulls S up.

That's it. You don't need a grand plan for success. You need to find the few things most likely to sink you, and quietly take them off the table before they get the chance.
