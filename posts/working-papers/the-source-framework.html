A diagnostic for preventing startup failure at the source.

By Terence Bumah
The SOURCE Framework™ and The SOURCE Canvas™
Working paper · 2025
Summary Most work on why startups fail looks at execution: the team, the product, the marketing. I think the more common cause sits earlier, in the opportunity itself, and it's visible before anyone writes code. SOURCE scores six properties of an opportunity (Severity, Occurrence, Urgency, Replicability, Competition, Emergence) and collapses them into three levers you can act on: Attention, Revenue, Growth. Where Porter's five forces judges a firm's position inside an existing industry, SOURCE judges the opportunity before there is a position to defend. The output is a diagnosis and a single next move, captured on the SOURCE Canvas.

1. The problem

Execution is not where most ideas die

Most strategies for business failure focus on execution: the team, product, marketing, sales. These strategies feel comforting, because execution can always be controlled and improved. But often the key problem is there before anyone writes any code. It is the opportunity itself, visible to anyone who looks closely.

There are really two questions about any startup venture or new product. Is this a problem whose economics allow a good business at all? And can my company win it? Founders spend nearly all their time on the second question and almost none on the first.

Failure usually follows a pattern: the problem was real, but nobody was in a hurry to solve it. Or people were in a hurry, but the solution was easy to copy. Or it worked, but the customer only needed it sparingly. None of these is an execution failure.

SOURCE is a framework I've developed to address failure from its "source" (no pun intended). It gives you a way of looking at new ideas from the outside. It scores six properties of an opportunity, and mostly things you cannot control. You can change your product by next quarter, but how often your customer has the problem is out of your hands.

2. The six properties

What SOURCE measures

The framework scores six properties of an opportunity, each out of 5. The name is a mnemonic for the six.

PropertyWhat it measuresScore 1 → 5
S - SeverityHow much damage the problem does when it happens.1 = mild annoyance → 5 = serious harm or cost
O - OccurrenceHow often the customer runs into it.1 = rarely → 5 = constantly
U - UrgencyHow fast the customer acts once it shows up.1 = someday → 5 = same day
R - ReplicabilityWhat it costs you to serve the next customer.1 = costly each time → 5 = near-zero
C - CompetitionWhether you can be different in a way you can defend.1 = commodity → 5 = structurally differentiated
E - EmergenceWhether the pool of customers is growing or shrinking.1 = shrinking → 5 = growing fast

Each property carries a strategy for raising it. Some can be moved by a design choice; others can only be moved by re-picking who you serve.

Severity. How much damage the problem does when it happens.

Raise it. Rarely a dial you can turn. Re-pick a segment where the same problem is acute, or bundle the small job into a critical workflow so failure carries that workflow's stakes.

Occurrence. How often the customer runs into it. Frequency is what builds habit, and habit is the cheapest retention there is.

Raise it. Find the recurring version of a one-off need, broaden the trigger to related jobs, or serve a segment that hits the problem more often.

Urgency. How fast the customer acts once the problem shows up.

Raise it. Tie it to a deadline already ticking, make the cost of waiting visible, and reach them at the moment of pain rather than before or after.

Replicability. What it costs you to serve the next customer.

Raise it. Productise the manual parts, let supply onboard itself, sell software or data instead of labour, and turn one-off delivery into a retained relationship.

Competition. What alternatives exist, and whether you can be different in a way you can defend.

Raise it. Copyable features do not count. Build something structural (exclusive supply, a data moat, network effects, switching costs) or retreat to a segment too awkward for the well-funded to chase.

Emergence. Whether the pool of customers is growing or shrinking. You only notice this one when it is missing.

Raise it. You cannot create a trend, only ride one. Position toward the segment where the tailwind is strongest, or re-anchor to an adjacent trend that is rising.

If you know Porter's five forces, this will look familiar. It is a similar framework, applied to an idea before there is an industry position to defend.

3. From six to three

The three levers

To turn a SOURCE score into an action plan, the six properties are collapsed into three concrete business problems that can be addressed through execution. Does the customer notice, do they buy, does it keep working?

Attention is Severity plus Occurrence. Severity gets you noticed. Occurrence keeps you there. Something agonising once a decade gets forgotten. Together the two set what acquiring customers will cost you, forever.

Revenue is Urgency plus Competition. Urgency decides whether they act at all. Competition decides whether they act with you.

Growth is Replicability plus Emergence. Replicability makes each new customer cheaper than the last. Emergence makes the pool deeper each year.

Each property is scored out of 5, so each lever comes out of 10. Do not add the three levers together. A business strong on Attention and weak on Revenue needs a different strategy from one with the reverse. Averaging hides the imbalance.

4. The method

Four steps

  1. Diagnose. Score each of the six properties out of 5. Score it cold, before you build the case for the idea.
  2. Collapse. Pair the properties into the three levers: Attention (S + O), Revenue (U + C), Growth (R + E), each out of 10.
  3. Locate. Find the lowest lever, then work out which of its two halves is short. Do not average; the pairing is what sets the strategy.
  4. Act. Take the one move that raises the weak half, then re-score to check the lever actually moved.

5. Raising a weak lever

The playbook

Find your lowest lever, then work out which of its two halves is short. The pairing sets the strategy, and every option below costs something.

If Attention is low

Low Severity with high Occurrence means they run into it constantly and don't care. Attach the trivial job to a consequential one, or monetise the traffic instead of the pain. You cannot market your way past indifference. High Severity with low Occurrence means it hurts, but rarely, so you end up buying the same customer twice. Capture more jobs per visit, find the weekly version of the need, or accept perpetual acquisition spend and price for it. Both low: find a segment where the same problem is acute, or move on.

If Revenue is low

Low Urgency with high Competition means you built something better and nobody is shopping. Fund distribution and manufacture demand, or re-enter through an adjacent product they already want. High Urgency with low Competition means demand is real but you're interchangeable. Find something the incumbents structurally can't match, or retreat to a segment too small or awkward for the well-funded to bother with. Both low: there is probably no business here yet.

If Growth is low

Low Replicability with high Emergence is a good market with expensive delivery. Change what you sell after the costly first sale, or monetise what delivery already generates, like payment history or usage data. High Replicability with low Emergence means you scale well into a closing market. Launch the next thing while this one still funds you. Both low: price it and fund it as a services business rather than forcing a shape onto it that it won't hold.

Where the numbers come from:

  • Severity 4. A leak or a dead boiler is a real problem: money, disruption and stress until it's fixed.
  • Occurrence 2. Most homes need a repair only a few times a year, so no habit ever forms.
  • Urgency 5. The moment something breaks, you're searching and paying that day.
  • Replicability 3. The app scales, but every new city needs its own tradespeople signed up by hand.
  • Competition 3. Vetting and instant booking help, but a competitor can copy both.
  • Emergence 3. Home repair is a steady need, not a growing one. Homes have always broken, and the number tracks the housing stock, which barely moves. No tailwind, no headwind.

That gives Attention 6, Revenue 8, Growth 6. Two levers are soft, and they share a cause: the customer comes once and leaves. Low Occurrence starves Attention: you solve a crisis, they vanish for months, and you pay to win them back. Low Replicability starves Growth for the same reason, since every sale is a fresh, expensive acquisition. Revenue is fine: urgent, real demand, with enough of an edge to convert it.

Now apply the playbook. For severe-but-rare Attention the move is to find the weekly version of the need or capture more jobs per visit, and here the same move fixes the Growth weakness too. So FixMate adds recurring home-maintenance plans: seasonal checks, gutter clearing, filter and alarm swaps, the small jobs people forget until they become the next crisis. A one-off repair becomes a standing relationship.

Watch what that does. Occurrence climbs from 2 to 4 as maintenance turns an annual panic into a regular touchpoint, lifting Attention to 8. Replicability climbs from 3 to 4, because a retained customer costs almost nothing to win back, lifting Growth to 7. Competition edges from 3 to 4, because a plan with your home's history in it is harder to leave than a one-off booking. Emergence does not move, because you cannot will the market to grow faster.

7. Scoring honestly and limitations

What the score is, and is not

Score the opportunity before you build the case for it. Once you've decided, every score bends toward the thesis you'd rather back. Score harshly. A generous score just delays the moment you find out.

Two properties are the ones people inflate most, so anchor them. For Emergence, ask plainly whether the problem is growing, stagnant, or shrinking: a growing need scores 4 or 5, a stagnant one 3, a shrinking one 1 or 2. For Competition, ask how hard you are to copy: a structurally differentiated product scores 4 or 5, a lightly differentiated one 3, and a commodity, where differentiation is almost impossible, scores 1 or 2.

SOURCE is a judgement tool, not a forecast. The scores are estimates made from the outside, and reasonable people will place them a point apart. What it buys you is honesty: the uncomfortable properties get into view before you've spent a year finding out. A strong score means the opportunity is worth building. Execution still has to happen; what SOURCE removes is the failure that was baked in before it ever began.
