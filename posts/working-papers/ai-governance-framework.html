A lifecycle approach to governing AI systems.

By Terence Bumah
The AI Lifecycle™
Working paper · 2026
Summary As organisations move from experimenting with AI to running it in business-critical processes, governance has to move past high-level principles. What you need is an operating framework: how AI systems get proposed, built, challenged, approved, deployed, monitored, changed and eventually retired. This paper sets out an AI System Development Lifecycle (AI-SDLC). It builds on established model governance and extends it for modern AI: generative models, third-party foundation models, retrieval-augmented generation, autonomous agents and AI-enabled decisions. It has two phases. Pre-production runs Planning, Development, Validation, Approval and Implementation. Post-production runs Monitoring, Revalidation, Change Management and Decommissioning. Incident Management sits across production, and governance controls span every stage. One rule holds it together: no AI system moves to the next stage until the requirements for the current stage are completed, evidenced and accepted. And the depth of governance should track the risk, so a high-risk system gets far more challenge, testing and oversight than a low-risk one.

1. The problem

Why AI needs a lifecycle

AI governance is usually written as principles: fairness, transparency, accountability, privacy, safety. Those matter, but a principle does not tell you how to govern a live system. The operational questions are more concrete. Who can propose an AI system? What has to be captured before development starts? Which systems need independent validation, and what testing? Who can approve deployment? What evidence do you keep? What happens when the underlying model changes, when an incident occurs, when it is time to retire the thing? A lifecycle turns principles into decision points, controls, responsibilities and evidence.

The aim is not to eliminate AI risk. You cannot, and you would not want to. The aim is to use AI while the risks are identified, understood, controlled, accepted and managed over time.

2. The object

Govern the system, not just the model

Traditional model governance looks at the model. Modern AI applications are systems, and risk can come from any part of them: data, foundation model, prompts, retrieval, tools, agents, guardrails, the application and the human decisions on top. A foundation model can behave perfectly while the application still fails, because retrieval returned the wrong document, a prompt produced unintended behaviour, an agent misused a tool, permissions exposed restricted data, a guardrail failed, users leaned too hard on the output, or the external provider quietly changed the model underneath you. So the thing you govern is the AI system and its intended use, not just the model at the centre of it.

3. First principles

Six principles

The lifecycle rests on six. Accountability: every system has a named business owner answerable for its use and its outcomes. Risk proportionality: requirements track the potential impact, so an internal productivity tool should not carry the same process as a system shaping credit, employment, healthcare or customer outcomes. Independent challenge: material systems get reviewed by people far enough from the build to see it straight. Evidence-based approval: approval rests on documented evidence, not an assurance that the system is "safe" or "accurate". Continuous governance: approval is not permanent, and a system has to keep showing it is fit for purpose. Traceability: you should be able to reconstruct what was built, why it was approved, what was deployed, how it performed, what changed and who authorised the changes.

4. The map

The lifecycle

Nine stages. Pre-production runs Planning to Development to Validation to Approval to Implementation. Post-production runs Monitoring, Revalidation and Change Management, ending in Decommissioning. Incident Management operates across production. Every stage ends at a gate, and you only pass once the minimum requirements are done.

Stage 1

Planning

Planning is the first gate. Before you spend real money, decide whether the use case is appropriate, establish who owns it, and understand its risk. The point is to know not just what technology is proposed, but how it will be used and what happens if it fails.

You define the use case: the business problem, the AI capability, the intended users, the decisions or actions it will drive, the benefits and outputs expected, and the uses that are permitted and explicitly prohibited. You assign ownership across business, product, technical and the relevant control functions, with final accountability sitting on a named business owner. You register the system in the AI inventory. You run an initial risk assessment across customer harm, financial, regulatory, fairness, privacy, security, reputational, operational and safety exposure. Then you classify the risk.

Tier 1 - High risk. The AI materially shapes consequential decisions or creates significant regulatory, financial, customer or safety exposure. Tier 2 - Moderate risk. The AI supports decisions or processes, but meaningful human oversight remains. Tier 3 - Low risk. The AI supports productivity or low-impact internal work.

Finally you identify what applies: laws, regulations, internal policy, data and privacy requirements, security standards, third-party terms and record-keeping obligations.

Gate to DevelopmentThe use case is defined, an owner assigned, the system registered, the initial risk assessment and classification done, applicable requirements identified, and any prohibited use ruled out. Output: an approved development proposal.

Stage 2

Development

Build the system to the approved requirements, with controls designed into the architecture rather than bolted on afterward.

You document the architecture: models, foundation-model providers, training and application data, prompts, retrieval, vector stores, APIs, tools, agents, guardrails and the points where a human can step in. You assess the data across source, quality, relevance, representativeness, permissions, privacy, lineage, retention and potential bias. You justify your model and provider choices, including build-versus-buy, versions and hosting, remembering that buying a third-party model does not move accountability onto the provider. You design the controls: access limits, prompt controls, content filters, grounding, output limits, human review, confidence thresholds, transaction limits, agent permissions and fallbacks. Before any testing, you set the evaluation method: the metrics, acceptance thresholds, test data, and the fairness, robustness, safety and security tests, along with the failure tolerances you will accept. And you write down the limitations honestly: known weaknesses, assumptions, unsupported uses, data and model limits, expected failure modes.

Gate to ValidationArchitecture documented, data assessed, model choice justified, controls implemented, evaluation method defined, developer testing done, limitations recorded, and the whole package complete enough for someone independent to review. Output: an AI system development package.

Stage 3

Validation

Independent evidence that the system is fit for its intended purpose, challenging both the technical performance and the business use.

Validation checks the conceptual design: is AI even appropriate here, is the architecture reasonable, are the assumptions justified, do the controls address the risks. It tests performance against the defined requirements, which depending on the application can mean accuracy, precision and recall, hallucination rate, retrieval accuracy, completeness, consistency, latency and reliability. It probes robustness under unusual inputs, incomplete or conflicting information, adversarial prompts, distribution shift and system failures. Where it matters, it tests fairness across populations and user groups, and it checks that users and decision-makers get enough to understand that AI is being used, the basis of important outputs, the uncertainty and the limits. It tests privacy and security: data leakage, prompt injection, unauthorised access, model manipulation, insecure integrations. It confirms the human-oversight mechanisms actually work, that users can challenge, override, escalate and spot bad behaviour. Finally it states the residual risk and grades the findings by severity, concluding: validated, validated with conditions, or not validated.

Gate to ApprovalRequired testing performed, results meeting the acceptance criteria or exceptions documented, material weaknesses identified, critical issues remediated, residual risk clearly stated, and an explicit conclusion on fitness for use. Output: an independent validation report.

Stage 4

Approval

Validation gives you an independent assessment. Approval is the moment the organisation decides whether it will accept the residual risk and authorise deployment.

The approver confirms that material findings are resolved, mitigated, formally accepted or written into deployment conditions. They take on the remaining technical, operational, regulatory and customer risks and the dependencies with eyes open. They confirm the controls, monitoring and human oversight are defined, and they set the conditions of use: who can use it, what is permitted and prohibited, the operating limits, the oversight and monitoring required, and how often it must be revalidated. Approval authority should match the risk tier.

Gate to ImplementationValidation complete, material issues addressed, residual risk understood, operating conditions and monitoring set, and the right authority has formally approved deployment. Output: an approval record.

Stage 5

Implementation

Make sure the system that goes live is the system that was validated and approved. You verify the production configuration matches approval: the model and version, the prompts, the data sources, the integrations, the controls. You complete production, integration, user-acceptance, security, access and control testing. Monitoring is live before real use begins. Users get the training, instructions, limits, escalation routes and disclosure requirements they need. Fallbacks are defined for when the AI is unavailable, the outputs turn unreliable, a provider fails or a control breaks. And you record the deployment: date, version, owner, approval reference, configuration.

Gate to MonitoringDeployment matches approval, testing is done, monitoring is operational, users are trained, fallbacks exist, and the configuration is recorded. Output: a production deployment record.

Stage 6

Monitoring

Governance does not end at go-live. Monitoring tells you whether the system still operates inside its approved boundaries. You watch performance (accuracy, hallucination, retrieval quality, error rates, reliability, latency), risk signals (harmful outputs, fairness indicators, privacy and security events, control failures), usage (volumes, unexpected or prohibited use, override and human-intervention rates), and change (to data, model versions, providers, prompts, integrations and the surrounding business process). Set thresholds: green for operating normally, amber when something needs investigation or remediation, red when it needs escalation, restriction or suspension. Monitoring then routes the system to continue, remediate, assess a change, revalidate, or suspend and retire.

OutputAn AI monitoring report or dashboard, feeding the change, revalidation, incident and decommissioning stages.

Stage 7

Revalidation

A periodic or event-driven reassessment of whether the system is still fit for purpose. Triggers include a scheduled review, a real drop in performance, a significant incident, a model or data change, an architecture or regulatory change, expansion into new use cases or user populations, or a major provider change. Revalidation revisits the relevant parts of the original validation: conceptual soundness, performance, robustness, fairness, security, controls, limitations, how well monitoring has worked, and whether the system still suits its intended use. It concludes: continue, continue with conditions, remediate, suspend or decommission.

OutputAn AI revalidation report.

Stage 8

Change management

Changes to an approved system should not slip uncontrolled risk back in, but not every change needs full revalidation, so you classify by materiality. A change might be a model upgrade or new foundation model, altered prompts, new datasets or retrieval sources, new tools or agent permissions, new integrations, a new customer population or a new business use. You assess whether it affects behaviour, risk profile, performance, controls or regulatory obligations, and classify it: a minor change needs limited testing and documentation, a material change needs targeted validation and approval, and a major change sends you back to the relevant earlier stages. You run regression and targeted testing, then approve and record it with version history.

Gate to productionImpact assessed, materiality set, testing and any required validation done, approval obtained, documentation and inventory updated. Output: a change assessment record.

Across production

Incident management

Incidents happen across production, not at a single point in the sequence. They include materially wrong outputs, harmful recommendations, discriminatory outcomes, disclosure of confidential information, prompt injection, unauthorised agent actions, serious control failures, unexpected model behaviour and provider outages. The process runs detect, contain, assess, escalate, investigate, remediate, validate, close. A material incident should also prompt questions about suspension, revalidation, regulatory reporting, customer remediation and change control, and its history should feed straight back into monitoring and future risk assessments.

Stage 9

Decommissioning

Retire systems that are no longer needed, supported or appropriate, and do it safely. You approve the retirement and record why. You identify what depends on the system: processes, applications, users, customers, downstream systems. You migrate them to a replacement or fallback where needed, then remove production access by disabling APIs, credentials, integrations, agents and endpoints. You handle the data under the right retention, archival and deletion rules, and you preserve the governance evidence: approvals, validation reports, monitoring records, change history, incidents and documentation. Then you mark the system decommissioned in the inventory.

Gate to closureThe system is offline, dependencies resolved, access removed, data obligations met, records retained and the inventory updated. Output: a decommissioning record.

Who does what

Operating model

Three lines of defence. The first line, the product, business and technology teams that build and own the system, identifies risks, meets the development standards, tests, documents, monitors and operates the controls. The second line, AI governance, risk, compliance, privacy, security and model risk, sets the standards and provides independent challenge, and for material systems the independent validation may sit here or in a dedicated function. The third line, internal audit, assesses whether the framework itself is well designed and working. The business owns the AI risk, governance sets the standards and challenges it, and audit provides independent assurance.

The payoff

The evidence chain

One of the most valuable things the framework produces is a continuous chain of evidence. For any production system you should be able to answer, in order: why does it exist (Planning), how was it built (Development), how do we know it works (Validation), who accepted the risk (Approval), what exactly was deployed (Implementation), how do we know it still works (Monitoring), when was it last independently reassessed (Revalidation), what has changed since approval (Change Management), what has failed (Incident Management), and what happened when it was retired (Decommissioning). That is an end-to-end audit trail.

The further ambition is to move this from documentation into a working system, where every AI system carries a live record (owner, risk tier, stage, evidence, findings, approvals, versions, monitoring, incidents, changes) and movement between stages is driven by workflow: planning requirements completed unlocks development, development evidence submitted unlocks validation, and so on, with monitoring events able to trigger change management, revalidation, incident handling or decommissioning automatically. The payoff is that you can see not just which AI systems you have, but who owns them, what risk they carry, whether they have been challenged, which version is running, how they are performing, and whether they are still approved for use.

Governance works best when decisions are explicit, responsibilities are clear, and the evidence exists before the risk is accepted.

Where this leaves you

Governance should not sit as a set of principles applied after the technology is already built. It belongs inside the lifecycle an AI system travels through, from Plan, Develop, Validate, Approve and Implement to Monitor, Revalidate, Change and Decommission, held together by risk classification, accountability, documentation, independent challenge, incident management and continuous oversight. The lesson from mature model governance carries straight over. Applied to AI, it gives you a way to build, deploy and scale with confidence rather than a way to slow everything down.
