A competitive strategy framework for building businesses that last.

By Terence Bumah
The ABCD Canvas
Working paper · 2026
Summary There is no shortage of competitive strategy frameworks. Porter's five forces map the structure of an industry. His generic strategies ask whether you win on cost or on differentiation. Blue Ocean says to stop fighting in crowded markets and create uncontested space. VRIO tests whether a resource is a real advantage or merely useful. Each of these explains something important, and each tends to leave you holding a diagnosis rather than a plan. ABCD is built to close that gap. It organises competitive strategy into four things a business can act on: Activating customers, Building loyalty, Competing long-term, Delivering at scale. It draws on the older frameworks, but arranges them as an order of operations.

1. The problem

Strategy that stops at analysis

Most strategy work explains the world well and says little about what to do next. The five forces tell you why an industry is hard to profit in, but not how to win it. Blue Ocean tells you to find uncontested space, but not how to reach it or hold it. VRIO tells you whether a resource counts as an advantage, but only once you already have the resource. A founder can read all of them and still not know which move comes first.

Meanwhile, plenty of businesses win early and then fade. They get a strong start, some real traction, a few good years, and then lose the ground they gained. Usually the cause is the same: they mistook a good start for a durable position, and never built the parts of the business that would have kept them ahead once competitors noticed.

2. The framework

What ABCD is

Here is a simple framework I use for building a business that lasts, and it spells ABCD: Activating customers, Building loyalty, Competing long-term, Delivering at scale. This framework helps you think about four things you need to succeed long-term. It takes the ideas the classic frameworks work with and puts them in the order a business actually faces them. First you have to get people to try you. Then you have to keep them. Then you have to stay ahead of the people who copy you. Then you have to reach enough of the market for any of it to matter. Each letter is a place a business can quietly fail, and each points to the older frameworks and playbooks that help you strengthen it.

Each letter is a stage in the life of a business, and a place it can fail. A strong business holds all four at once.

3. Activating

A: Activating Customers

The first job is to get people to actually try you, not just hear of you. Activation is not awareness. It is the first time a customer reaches the result they came for, the moment the product does for them what it promised. Until that happens, everything upstream is only cost.

The work here is choosing the channels that get you to that moment. There are four broad routes, and most businesses lean on one or two.

  • Offline. Retail, events, field sales, and word of mouth. Slower to scale, but strong where the product needs to be seen or trusted in person.
  • Online. Search, content, paid acquisition, and marketplaces. Measurable and fast to test, but crowded and often expensive.
  • Strategic partnerships. Reaching customers through another company that already serves them. You trade margin or control for immediate access to a base you would otherwise have to build.
  • Influencers. Borrowing trust someone else has already built with an audience, so the recommendation carries weight your own advertising would not.

Choose well. The right mix depends on where your customers already are, what they cost to reach, and how much explaining the product needs before someone will try it. A product that sells itself in one sentence can lean on online and influencers; one that needs to be demonstrated usually needs offline or partnerships first.

4. Building

B: Building Loyalty

Winning a customer once is not the same as keeping them, and it is far cheaper to keep one than to find a new one. A business that cannot hold on to the people it wins has to keep spending to replace them. Loyalty starts with value, and value starts with solving a real problem.

This is where SOURCE helps. It scores whether the problem is severe and frequent enough to earn a place in the customer's routine. A product that solves a rare or trivial problem will struggle to hold anyone, regardless of how well it is made.

Once the value is real, the product has to be sticky, so that staying is easier than leaving. The way to get there is to keep improving the product against what customers actually do, and I use a simple loop for this called ABCD Value Differentiation: Analyse requirements, Build product, Check feedback, Distribute to market, then repeat. Each pass fits the product a little more closely to the customer, and a product that fits is a product people stay with.

StepWhat you do
A - Analyse requirementsWork out what the customer actually needs the product to do.
B - Build productBuild the smallest version that meets that need.
C - Check feedbackPut it in front of real users and watch what they do, not just what they say.
D - Distribute to marketShip the improved version to your market, then start the loop again.

Stickiness comes from delivering the result consistently, from habit, and from value that accumulates the longer a customer stays, such as their history, data, or setup inside the product. The test is simple: when a cheaper or newer alternative appears, does the customer have a reason to stay that is stronger than the reason to go.

5. Competing

C: Competing Long-term

Anyone can be copied. The question is how quickly, and at what cost to whoever tries. What slows a competitor down is a moat: an advantage that is structurally hard to reproduce.

VRIO is the test for whether you have one. An advantage has to be Valuable, Rare, hard to Imitate, and something the Organisation can actually exploit. If a supposed advantage fails any of these, it is not a moat, and you should not plan as though it is.

TestThe question
V - ValuableDoes it let you serve customers better or at lower cost?
R - RareDo few or no competitors already have it?
I - ImitableIs it costly or slow for others to copy or substitute?
O - OrganisationIs the business set up to actually exploit it?

The common moats are a brand people trust, a network that gets more useful as it grows, costs rivals cannot match, proprietary technology or data, switching costs that make leaving painful, or a high barrier to entry that keeps most competitors out entirely. Without at least one, today's lead is temporary, and you should assume someone is already working to take it.

6. Delivering

D: Delivering at Scale

A good product that few people can reach loses to an average product that everyone can. Delivering at scale is how you grow three things at once: customers, revenue, and reach. The levers differ by business, but they tend to fall into a few groups.

  • More customers. Add channels, enter new segments or markets, and widen the top of the funnel.
  • More revenue per customer. Raise prices where you can, sell more to existing customers, and move one-off sales into recurring relationships.
  • More reach per customer. Build loops where existing customers bring new ones, through referrals, sharing, or a product that becomes more visible the more it is used.

Own the channel where you can. Prefer channels you own over channels you rent. A business that depends on a single channel it does not control, an ad platform, an app store, or one large client, is one policy change away from trouble. Durable distribution is as much an asset as the product itself.

7. The map

How the classic frameworks fit

ABCD does not replace the older frameworks. It puts them to work, each in the stage where it is most useful.

LetterThe questionFrameworks that help
A - ActivatingHow do we get people to try us?Go-to-market and channel strategy, Blue Ocean for new demand
B - Building loyaltyHow do we keep them?SOURCE for a real problem, ABCD Value Differentiation for stickiness, jobs-to-be-done
C - Competing long-termHow do we stay ahead once copied?VRIO, Porter's generic strategies, barriers to entry
D - Delivering at scaleHow do we reach enough of the market?Growth loops, channel economics, owned versus rented reach

8. The method

How you can make this work

Score your business out of five on each of the four: activation, loyalty, competition, delivery. The lowest score is usually where the real risk sits. A business that activates well but keeps no one spends everything it earns finding replacements. One with loyal customers but no moat will lose them the moment a stronger competitor arrives. One with a great product and weak delivery never reaches the scale that would have made it matter. Work the weakest letter first, then score again. Lasting success comes from keeping all four strong enough that no single one becomes the thing that undoes you.
