A loop for launching products that customers keep using.

By Terence Bumah
The ABCD Value Differentiation Loop
Working paper · 2026
Summary A launch is a bet that the product you built is the product people want. Value Differentiation keeps that bet small and repeatable. You run four stages in order, Analyse requirements, Build product, Check feedback, Distribute to market, and you do not pass time or money to the next stage until the current one clears a gate.

1. The problem

Launches fail on assumptions

Most products are built over months on a guess about what customers want. The team commits to the guess, builds the full thing, and launches once at full scale. The result comes back after the money has been spent, and if the guess was wrong there is little runway left to correct it.

The cause is rarely the quality of the idea. Most often the assumptions underneath it were never tested while testing them was still cheap. A founder assumes the problem is worth solving, assumes the product solves it, assumes people will use it, and assumes there is a way to reach them at a cost the business can carry. Each of those is a separate bet. Launched together they fail together, and you cannot tell which one broke.

The way to reduce that risk is to separate the bets and settle them one at a time, spending only enough to learn whether each holds before you fund the next.

2. The framework

Value Differentiation

Here is a simple framework I use, called ABCD Value Differentiation. It takes a founder from a raw idea to a product people keep using and pay for, and it spells ABCD: Analyse requirements, Build product, Check feedback, Distribute to market.

You run the four stages in order and then run them again. Every stage has the same internal shape. You gather information, you prioritise it, you act on the top item, and then you reach a gate. The gate is a plain test of whether the stage has done its job. You do not move to the next stage until the current one passes. Each pass through the loop settles one of the assumptions from the launch, at a cost you choose in advance.

3. Foundations

What this builds on

The parts of this loop are borrowed. Lean Startup gave us Build-Measure-Learn and validated learning, the practice of treating a product as an experiment rather than a finished bet. Agile and Scrum gave us user stories and short iterations, so that work ships in small increments a customer can react to. Jobs-to-be-done gave us a way to describe a requirement in terms of what a customer is trying to accomplish. The traction-channels method gave us a way to test acquisition before scaling it. MoSCoW gave us a language for deciding what is a must have and what is not.

What Value Differentiation adds is the ordering and the discipline around it. It puts these methods into one sequence for a launch, forces a prioritisation step inside every stage, and places a risk gate between each stage and the next spend. It reads that gate the way a risk officer reads a control, using KPIs to confirm value is being delivered and KRIs to catch value leaking before it becomes a loss. A founder using it is running the same experiments the established methods describe, held to a standard of proof before each new commitment of money.

4. Analyse

A: Analyse Requirements

The first bet is that there is a problem worth solving. You settle it by gathering pain points from real users through interviews and observation, and confirming the problem exists before you build against it. This is the work that SOURCE covers, and the same instinct behind jobs-to-be-done and Lean Startup customer discovery. You are looking for what a specific person cannot get done today and how much that failure costs them.

A founder rarely has one problem to solve. More often there are several, and the first mistake is trying to address all of them in the first product. So you prioritise. Rank the pain points by how much each hurts, how often it happens, how many people have it, and whether they will pay to remove it. The one that scores highest becomes the requirement you build against.

Examples of pain points you might land on:

  • Income is irregular, so setting money aside never happens.
  • The loan application is abandoned at the income-verification step.
  • Merchant settlement takes three days and costs sales.
  • Users do not trust a new wallet with more than a small balance.

Gate. Is the top pain point severe, frequent, and something people will pay to remove. If it is not, keep gathering or drop the idea. Failing here costs a few weeks of conversation. Discovering it after launch costs the build.

5. Build

B: Build Product

The second bet is that your product solves the problem you chose. You translate the priority pain point into user stories, the short statements of what a user wants and why, then into the features those stories require. This is Agile work, and the user story keeps the feature tied to a real need rather than to a preference inside the team.

Not every feature is a must have. A founder who treats them all as essential builds for a year and launches something too large to have been tested. MoSCoW is how you avoid that. Sort the features into must have, where the product fails without them, should have, could have, and will not have this release. The first version is the must haves and nothing else. It is the smallest product that lets one user complete the core job from start to finish.

Examples of user stories and the features they require:

  • As a driver, I want each payout rounded up automatically, so that I save without having to decide. Feature: auto round-up into a savings pot.
  • As a first-time borrower, I want my application pre-filled from my bank data, so that I do not re-enter details. Feature: pre-filled application.
  • As a merchant, I want funds available the same day, so that I can restock. Feature: instant settlement wallet.

Gate. Can a real user finish the core job end to end with this version. If they cannot, the answer is to remove scope until the core job works, rather than to add more around a job that does not yet hold.

6. Check

C: Check Feedback

The third bet is that people will use what you built. You settle it by putting the first version in front of real users and reading what happens. This is Build-Measure-Learn, and the measure step is where most launches are too vague to learn anything. You fix that by deciding in advance what you will measure and what result would count as success or failure.

Set two kinds of number. KPIs tell you whether the product is delivering the value you designed it for. KRIs tell you where that value is leaking, early enough to act. The risk indicators matter as much as the performance ones, because a product can post a healthy activation number and still be losing users at a step nobody is watching.

  • KPIs, value delivered. Activation rate, second-week retention, conversion through each step.
  • KRIs, value leaking. Share of users who withdraw within a week, drop-off concentrated at a single step, support-ticket volume, complaint and fraud rates.

Read the numbers, then act on the one signal that most threatens the core value. The decision at the end of this stage is whether to carry on with the current product, change direction while keeping what you have learned, or stop.

Gate. Are the KPIs above the thresholds you set and the KRIs below them. If they are not, the leak gets fixed before any money goes into growth. Spending on growth while the product loses the users it wins raises the cost of every user without raising the number who stay.

7. Distribute

D: Distribute to Market

The fourth bet is that there is a way to reach these users at a cost the business can carry. You settle it the way the traction-channels method suggests, by listing the channels open to you and testing a few at small scale before committing a budget.

  • Channel partnership. Place the product inside a platform that already has your users, such as a delivery app whose drivers you are building for.
  • Direct acquisition. Paid and organic online channels pointed at a landing page.
  • Referral. Give existing users a reason to bring the next one.
  • Community and influencers. Reach a defined audience through people they already follow.
  • Sales-led. For business customers, direct outreach and paid pilots.

Judge each channel on the cost to acquire a customer against the value that customer returns over time. Keep the channels where the value clears the cost and stop the ones where it does not.

Gate. Does at least one channel return more than it costs, and does it do so again when you repeat it. A channel that pays back once may have been luck. A channel that pays back on repetition is something you can scale.

When a channel clears the gate, the loop does not end. What you learn about who converts and why becomes the input to the next round of Analyse, and you run the sequence again on the next requirement.

8. The method

Using it

Run one loop at a time and keep each pass short. Before a stage begins, write down the single assumption you are testing and the gate result that would clear it. Judge the stage against that gate. When a stage fails, the move is back a step rather than forward.

A failed gate early in the loop is a cheap failure. Skipping the gate does not remove the failure. It moves it to after the launch, where correcting it costs the most. The founders who get into trouble are usually the ones who skipped a gate because the idea felt obvious.
