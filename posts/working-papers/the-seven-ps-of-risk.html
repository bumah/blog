A framework for managing risk simpler, smarter and safer.

By Terence Bumah
The Seven Ps of Risk
Working paper · 2026

Risk in financial services looks complex because it shows up everywhere: across teams, systems, products, regulations and external events. But most of the risks that matter trace back to a small number of sources. I group them into seven: people, processes, platforms, products, policies, protection and politics.

I call these the Seven Ps of risk.

This is a practical lens, not a formal taxonomy. Some of the Ps are where risk originates, some are where it is carried, and some are where it is controlled. The task for any risk leader is threefold: understand each of the seven, unify them into one connected view, and master oversight across all of them. Get that right and you have the strongest foundation for long-term resilience.

1. People: the people who run the bank

Every institution depends on people making the right decisions, following the right procedures and escalating the right issues at the right time.

Risk rises when responsibilities are unclear, training is thin, teams are overloaded, work happens in silos, or critical knowledge lives in someone's head rather than in the system. People risk runs deeper than misconduct or error. It is capability, accountability, culture and judgement. Done well, it looks like clear ownership, defined responsibilities, and knowledge that is captured rather than assumed.

2. Processes: the work you run

Banks run thousands of critical processes across risk, compliance, lending, operations, finance and governance.

When those processes are manual, inconsistent or spread across spreadsheets and inboxes, work becomes slower and harder to control. Delays, missed approvals, weak hand-offs and thin evidence trails are usually process failures before they become risk events. The strong version is consistent, documented workflows with clear approvals and a reliable evidence trail.

3. Platforms: the systems you operate

Modern banks run on many platforms, from core banking systems and risk engines to workflow tools, document stores and reporting systems.

When those platforms do not connect, information fragments. Teams spend their time reconciling data, hunting for records and building manual workarounds. Platform risk is rarely outright system failure. More often it is disconnected systems creating operational weakness. The strong version is connected systems and a single, trusted view of the data that matters.

4. Products: the products you build, sell and govern

Every financial product carries risk, and often several kinds at once: credit, market, conduct, operational, regulatory and reputational.

The more complex the product, the more its lifecycle, controls, approvals, documentation and customer impact need active governance. Products become dangerous when governance falls behind innovation, distribution or changing customer behaviour. Handled well, product governance keeps pace with the product, from approval through to ongoing review.

5. Policies: the rules you must follow, and prove you follow

Policies turn regulation, standards and internal expectations into how the institution is meant to operate.

But a policy only reduces risk when people can find it, understand it and apply it in their daily work. When policies are scattered, out of date or disconnected from workflows, they become documents people cite after an event rather than controls built into the work. Policies are where risk is meant to be controlled, which is exactly why weak policy management quietly creates it. The strong version is current, accessible policies mapped to the controls and workflows they govern.

6. Protection: the data, access and trust you must safeguard

Banks hold some of the most sensitive information in the economy: customer identities, financial records, credit histories, transaction data, employee data and confidential business information.

Risk rises when data is poorly classified, access is not properly controlled, systems are not secure, or sensitive information is used without clear governance. This is where privacy and cybersecurity meet: privacy governs how data may be used, security protects the systems that hold it, and customer trust depends on both.

In an AI-enabled world, protection also means responsible use: who can access what, which data can be used, how outputs are controlled and how sensitive information is kept safe. Handled well, it means classified data, controlled access, secure systems, and clear rules for how information and AI are used.

7. Politics: the external environment you must prepare for

Banks do not operate in isolation.

Interest rates, inflation, currency movements, regulation, sanctions, elections, conflict, trade policy and broader geopolitical instability can shift an institution's risk profile quickly. These external pressures feed straight into credit quality, liquidity, market risk, capital, customer behaviour and operational resilience.

This is why stress testing matters. It forces the institution to ask not only what is happening today, but what could happen next, and whether the bank could withstand it. The strong version is stress scenarios that reflect the real environment, reviewed often enough to stay relevant.

Where the financial risks sit

You may have noticed that credit, market, liquidity and capital risk are not Ps of their own. That is deliberate. These financial risks, along with newer concerns such as model risk, third-party risk and financial crime, do not live in a single box. They flow through the Seven Ps. A credit loss can begin as a people problem, a process gap, a platform blind spot or an external shock. The Seven Ps are where these risks are created, carried and controlled.

The real risk is fragmentation

The challenge is that these sources of risk are deeply connected, but almost always managed separately.

People sit in one place. Processes in another. Platforms hold different pieces of the data. Product governance happens somewhere else. Policies live in document repositories. Protection is treated as a specialist domain. External politics is assessed periodically, but rarely connected to day-to-day decisions.

That fragmentation is the real risk. You cannot master what you cannot see in one place.

Unifying the Seven Ps is what makes real control possible. More reporting, more committees and more manual checks will not get you there. What does is connecting the Seven Ps into one clear view of risk, so that people, processes, platforms, products, policies, protection and politics can be seen and managed together.

The question for CROs

For a CRO, the practical question is simple: how strong is your institution across each of the Seven Ps?

Do you have clear ownership? Do you have reliable data? Do you have connected workflows? Do you have meaningful KRIs? Do you have controls that work in practice, not just on paper? Do you have stress scenarios that reflect the world your institution actually operates in?

A CRO should be able to see, measure and improve risk oversight across all seven areas: tracking the right indicators, spotting weak points early, and having a plan to strengthen governance, control and mitigation. That is how risk and compliance moves from fragmented oversight to genuine control.

Bringing the Seven Ps together

Each of the Seven Ps matters on its own. The problem is that in most banks they live apart. People sit in one system, policies in another, processes in a fourth, and the evidence that proves any of it is scattered across inboxes, shared drives and spreadsheets. Risk teams spend more time assembling the picture than acting on it, managers get a view that is already out of date, and leaders are asked to sign off on numbers they cannot fully trace.

Closing that gap is the whole point of the framework. Bring the Seven Ps into one connected view, and risk and compliance stop operating as fragments and start operating as one.

One connected view

The Seven Ps, brought together

Simpler  ·  Smarter  ·  Safer

For the teams doing the work, it is simpler. People, policies, processes, controls and evidence sit in one place, with a clear owner and a clear status on everything. There is no hunting for the latest version and no rebuilding the same report every quarter.

For the people asking questions, it is smarter. You can query across the whole picture, run your reports on demand, and analyse where the real exposure sits rather than waiting on a manual roll-up. Trends, gaps and overdue items surface early, while there is still time to act.

For the institution, it is safer. Every change is versioned and on the audit trail, so what you present to regulators, auditors and the board is traceable back to source. Nothing depends on one person's memory or one team's spreadsheet.

That is what managing risk simpler, smarter and safer looks like in practice: one place where the Seven Ps connect, where risk teams, managers and leaders work from the same trusted view, and where oversight becomes something you can run rather than chase.
