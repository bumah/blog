Innovation is rarely constrained by a shortage of ideas. It is constrained by the discipline required to convert an idea into something the market values and adopts. That challenge is common to both the entrepreneur launching an independent venture and the intrapreneur developing a new proposition within an established organisation. The starting conditions differ; the discipline required to succeed does not.

The ABCD framework organises that discipline into four gated stages — Analyse, Build, Check, and Distribute. Each stage carries a defined set of activities and a gate that must be cleared before progressing to the next. The model is deliberately governance-led: it is designed to ensure that capital, time, and organisational attention are committed only where the evidence justifies it. Applied consistently, ABCD replaces optimism and momentum with a repeatable, defensible basis for decision-making that any team — or any board — can follow.

A — Analyse

Every venture should begin with the problem rather than the proposed solution. The most frequent cause of failure is the pursuit of a problem that is either insufficiently painful or insufficiently widespread to sustain a business. The Analyse stage exists to establish, on evidence, that a genuine opportunity is present.

Key activities:

  • Define the problem precisely and identify the segment that experiences it
  • Assess the severity of the problem and the alternatives customers currently rely on
  • Size the addressable market and evaluate the competitive landscape
  • Validate demand through direct engagement with prospective customers
  • Outline a preliminary view of the commercial or value model

Gate to Build: a clearly articulated, evidence-based problem and a defensible view of the proposed solution. Where the problem and its owners cannot be stated with confidence, the venture should not proceed.

For the entrepreneur, this stage determines whether the opportunity warrants the commitment of personal capital and reputation. For the intrapreneur, the same market test applies, alongside a second consideration: strategic fit and the case for internal sponsorship. Both must evidence a real problem; the intrapreneur must additionally demonstrate that it merits priority against competing organisational demands. The common risk is over-analysis — treating research as a substitute for the decision to proceed. Analysis should build sufficient conviction to act, and no more.

B — Build

With the opportunity validated, the objective shifts to creating a minimum viable version of the solution — the smallest offering that meaningfully addresses the core problem for a real user. The purpose of building is not to demonstrate the complete vision, but to generate the evidence and learning that planning alone cannot provide.

Key activities:

  • Define the minimum scope that resolves the core problem
  • Develop the solution efficiently and at pace
  • Bring it to a standard that genuine users can adopt
  • Establish, in advance, the metrics by which performance will be judged

Gate to Check: a functioning initial version in the hands of real users, supported by a clear means of measuring outcomes.

The entrepreneur typically builds under significant resource constraint — a discipline that enforces focus and speed. The intrapreneur usually operates with greater resources but faces countervailing pressures: established processes, legacy systems, and a cultural preference for polish and risk mitigation ahead of market contact. In both settings, the imperative is the same — to place a workable solution in front of real users quickly, and to resist the temptation to perfect an offering the market has yet to endorse.

C — Check

The Check stage subjects the solution to the evidence of actual use. Real users engage with the product, and their behaviour — not the team's expectations — informs the assessment. This is the stage at which objectivity is most difficult and most essential, given the natural tendency to favour confirming signals.

Key activities:

  • Observe how real users engage with the solution
  • Capture qualitative feedback and corroborate it with usage data
  • Measure outcomes against the expectations set during the Build stage
  • Reach a clear decision: continue, refine, or discontinue

Gate to Distribute: credible evidence that the solution resolves the problem and commands genuine demand. Where that evidence is absent, the appropriate response is to return to Analyse or Build rather than to advance a proposition the market has not validated.

For the entrepreneur, disciplined evaluation is a matter of commercial survival. For the intrapreneur, an additional dynamic applies: internal commitments, allocated budgets, and reputational considerations can create pressure to declare success prematurely. Organisations may inadvertently reward the appearance of progress over an honest assessment. Protecting the integrity of this stage is therefore critical; a proposition advanced on internal politics rather than market evidence carries a materially higher cost of failure.

D — Distribute

A well-designed solution delivers no value until it reaches its intended users. Distribution encompasses the channels, marketing, sales, and partnerships through which the offering reaches the market — and it is the stage most frequently underestimated. It should be planned deliberately from the outset, not addressed once the product is complete.

Key activities:

  • Identify the channels that effectively reach the target segment
  • Develop the marketing, sales, and partnership approach to match
  • Measure the cost and return of each channel
  • Concentrate investment in the channels that perform and withdraw from those that do not

Gate to Scale: repeatable, economically viable routes to customers. Once the organisation can reliably and cost-effectively reach its market, the proposition is ready to scale.

For the entrepreneur, distribution is decisive; a strong product without a route to market has no commercial future. The intrapreneur holds a distinct and often underexploited advantage — an established customer base, existing channels, a recognised brand, and a functioning sales capability. The challenge is less about building distribution than about securing the internal alignment to direct existing capabilities toward the new proposition. The requirement is common to both: distribution must be planned early and treated as integral to the venture.

Why the gates matter

The gates are the framework's defining feature. Articulating four stages is straightforward; the discipline lies in declining to advance until each gate is genuinely cleared. That discipline compels an honest assessment before further capital and time are committed, and it converts venture-building from an individual judgement call into a transparent process that a team, a sponsor, or a board can scrutinise and support.

ABCD is not a linear exercise completed once. It is a continuous loop: the insight generated at Distribute informs the next cycle of Analyse, and each iteration refines the proposition and strengthens its fit with the market. Whether building an independent venture or a new proposition within an established organisation, sustained success rarely belongs to those with the strongest initial idea. It belongs to those who execute this cycle with pace and rigour — and who consistently decline to bypass a gate.
