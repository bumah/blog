Innovation is rarely short of ideas. What it's short of is the discipline to turn an idea into something the market actually values and adopts. That's true whether you're launching an independent venture or building a new proposition inside an established company. The starting conditions are different; the discipline that decides whether either one works is not.

The way I organise that discipline is a framework I call ABCD: Analyse, Build, Check and Delivery. Each stage has its own activities and a clear test to pass before moving to the next. The point of running it this way is to stop capital, time and attention going in before the evidence justifies it. Done consistently, it replaces optimism and momentum with something a team, or a board, can actually follow.

A - Analyse

Every venture should start with the problem, not the solution. The most common reason they fail is chasing a problem that isn't painful enough, or widespread enough, to carry a business. Analyse is where you prove, on evidence, that a real opportunity is there.

What this stage covers:

  • Define the problem precisely and work out who actually has it
  • Gauge how severe it is and what people use to cope today
  • Size the market and look hard at the competition
  • Test demand by talking to real prospective customers
  • Sketch a first view of how it would make money

What you want out of it: a problem you can state clearly and back with evidence, and a solution you can defend. If you can't name the problem and whose it is with confidence, the venture shouldn't proceed.

For the entrepreneur, this decides whether the opportunity is worth their own capital and reputation. For the intrapreneur the same market test applies, plus one more: does it fit the strategy, and can they get it sponsored internally? Both need real evidence of a real problem; the internal version also has to earn priority over everything else competing for attention. The trap I watch for in my own work is over-analysis, using research to put off the decision. You want enough conviction to act, and no more.

B - Build

Once the problem looks real, build the smallest version of the solution that genuinely addresses it for a real user. You build to learn. Planning only takes you so far; a rough version in someone's hands tells you things a plan never will.

What this stage covers:

  • Work out the smallest scope that solves the core problem
  • Build it quickly, without over-engineering
  • Get it to a state a real user will actually adopt
  • Decide up front how you'll measure whether it's working

What you want out of it: a working first version in real users' hands, with a clear way to measure what happens next.

Entrepreneurs usually build under real constraint, which is useful: it forces focus and speed. Intrapreneurs tend to have more resources but more drag too, established processes, legacy systems, and a culture that wants polish and caution before anything touches the market. I've been on both sides of that, and the job is the same either way: get a working version in front of real users fast, and resist the urge to perfect something the market hasn't endorsed yet.

C - Check

Then you let real use be the judge. People use the thing, and their behaviour, not your hopes for it, tells you where it stands. This is the hardest stage to stay honest in, because everyone naturally wants to believe the signals that confirm the idea.

What this stage covers:

  • Watch how real users actually engage with it
  • Take their feedback and check it against the usage data
  • Compare what's happening to the targets you set at Build
  • Make a call: keep going, change it, or stop

What you want out of it: real evidence that it solves the problem and that demand is there. When that evidence isn't there, the right move is back to Analyse or Build, not onward.

For the entrepreneur, judging this honestly is survival. For the intrapreneur there's an extra pull: budgets are committed, reputations are attached, and it gets tempting to declare success early. I've seen organisations reward the look of progress over an honest read. This is the stage I protect most, because a proposition carried on internal politics instead of market evidence just fails more expensively later.

D - Delivery

A good solution is worth nothing until it reaches the people it's for. Delivery is the channels, marketing, sales and partnerships that get it there, and it's the stage I see underestimated most often. Plan for it from the start rather than bolting it on once the product is done.

What this stage covers:

  • Find the channels that actually reach the people you're after
  • Build the marketing, sales and partnership approach to match
  • Measure what each channel costs and what it returns
  • Put money behind what works and pull it from what doesn't

What you want out of it: routes to customers that repeat and pay their way. Once you can reach the market reliably and cost-effectively, it's ready to scale.

For the entrepreneur, delivery decides it: a strong product with no route to market goes nowhere. The intrapreneur has an advantage that often goes unused, an existing customer base, live channels, a known brand and a working sales team. There the challenge is less about building delivery and more about getting internal alignment to aim what already exists at the new proposition. The rule holds either way: plan delivery early and treat it as part of the venture, not an afterthought.

Why the discipline matters

The tests between the stages are the whole point. Naming four stages is easy. The discipline is refusing to move on until a stage has genuinely earned it. That forces an honest look before more capital and time go in, and it turns venture-building from a gut call into something a team, a sponsor or a board can see and challenge.

ABCD isn't run once and filed away. It's a loop: what you learn at Delivery feeds the next round of Analyse, and each pass sharpens the proposition and its fit with the market. Whether it's your own venture or a new product inside a big company, the wins rarely go to whoever had the best first idea. They go to whoever runs this cycle with pace and honesty, and refuses to skip a step.
