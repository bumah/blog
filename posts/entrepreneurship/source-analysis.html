Most business ideas fail for a reason that was visible before a single line of code was written. Not a flaw in execution — a structural property of the opportunity itself.

The problem was real, but nobody was in a hurry to solve it. Or people were in a hurry, but the solution was easy for anyone to copy. Or it worked, but the customer only needed it once a year, so every sale had to be won from scratch.

None of these are visible from inside an idea you like. SOURCE Analysis is a way of looking at your idea from the outside. It scores six properties of an opportunity — never the product, the team, or the plan. You can change your product tomorrow. You cannot change how often your customer has the problem. The output isn't a go/no-go verdict; it's an honest description of what running the business will actually feel like.

Score each from 1 to 5. Ten minutes, done honestly, before you read any further into your own plan.

The three levers

Six numbers are too many to act on. You cannot walk into Monday morning with a list of six things to fix. But the six aren't independent — they pair up, because each pair answers one link in the chain commerce runs on: does the customer notice, do they buy, does it keep working.

LeverFormulaQuestionIf this lever is low
AttentionS + OIs the problem in the customer's head?You'll spend every year buying awareness you can't earn

Attention. Severity gets you noticed — a bad enough problem creates its own awareness. Occurrence keeps you there. Neither works alone: something agonising once a decade is forgotten, something trivial every day is background noise.

Revenue. Urgency determines whether they act at all. Competition determines whether they act with you. High urgency and no differentiation means demand goes to whoever is cheapest; differentiation and no urgency means a beautiful product nobody is shopping for.

Growth. Replicability makes each additional customer cheaper than the last. Emergence makes the pool of customers deeper each year. Cheap scaling into a shrinking market gets you nowhere.

Each lever scores out of 10. Don't sum all three — a business strong on Attention and weak on Revenue needs the opposite strategy to one strong on Revenue and weak on Attention. Averaging erases the only thing you scored for.

Worked example: Klook

Klook is a travel platform for booking theme-park tickets, transfers and day tours — the things you do after you land. Founded 2014; by 2025, roughly $3bn in annual bookings and filing to list in New York.

Where those numbers come from:

  • Severity 2. A badly-booked or missed day tour is an annoyance, not a crisis — you lose a little time or money on holiday, nothing that follows you home.
  • Occurrence 2. People travel abroad once or twice a year. The problem barely recurs, so no habit ever forms.
  • Urgency 4. Once you've landed and need a transfer or a ticket, you're searching and paying today — the intent is immediate.
  • Replicability 3. The software scales, but every new city needs local operators, inventory and languages signed up by hand. Real cost per market.
  • Competition 4. In 2014, instant mobile confirmation for tours and activities was genuinely new — the alternative was paper vouchers and hotel desks.
  • Emergence 5. Asian outbound travel and smartphone booking were both accelerating hard. About as strong as a tailwind gets.

Which gives Attention 4, Revenue 8, Growth 8 — and a decade later, 6, 8 and 7.

People would buy this, the market was expanding, and nobody was thinking about it.

That sentence predicted the next eleven years. Weak Attention is a permanent tax, not a temporary gap. Klook's growth strategy became a list of distribution deals — Google, TikTok, search. Its IPO filing names reliance on social traffic as a risk. Gross profit has sat around 11% of transaction value, against 15%+ for Airbnb and 20%+ for Uber.

The bars tell you which lever is short. They don't tell you why — and the why determines what you can do about it.

Both halves of the Attention lever were structurally weak. A badly-booked day tour is an annoyance, not a crisis. And people travel abroad once or twice a year — you cannot make them go more often.

That matters, because if only one had been weak, a move existed. Both weak means neither move is available. So Klook built the business around the score instead: permanent acquisition spend, higher-stakes jobs like transfers and rail passes, more bookings squeezed out of each trip. Attention moved from 4 to roughly 6 over a decade. The take rate never recovered.

The web also shows two declines. Competition fell as instant confirmation and mobile booking became standard. Emergence fell as travel normalised post-pandemic. Neither is failure — decay is scheduled from day one for any differentiation that works and any tailwind that arrives.

What each lever failure means

Revenue is existential. Low Revenue is a this-month problem, not a someday problem.

Attention is a tax. Low Attention sets your margin ceiling permanently. Plan the cost structure around it from day one, rather than waiting for retention that isn't coming.

Growth is a deadline. Low Growth means you have runway, but only if you spend it building the next thing rather than polishing this one.

Diagnostic playbook

Find your lowest lever, then identify which of its two halves is short. That pairing determines the strategy.

If Attention is low

  • Low Severity, high Occurrence — they run into it constantly and don't care. Attach the trivial job to a consequential one and borrow its severity, or monetise the traffic instead of the pain. You cannot market your way past indifference.
  • High Severity, low Occurrence — it hurts, but rarely, so no habit forms and you buy the same customer twice. Capture more jobs per visit, find the weekly version of the need, or fund acquisition forever on purpose.
  • Both low — find a segment where the same problem is acute, or move on.

If Revenue is low

  • Low Urgency, high Competition — you built something better and nobody is shopping. Fund distribution forever and manufacture demand, or re-enter through an adjacent product they already want.
  • High Urgency, low Competition — demand is real, you're interchangeable. Find something the incumbent structurally can't match, or retreat to a segment too small or awkward for the well-funded to chase.
  • Both low — you have an observation about the world, not a business.

If Growth is low

  • Low Replicability, high Emergence — good market, expensive delivery. Change what you sell after the costly first sale so acquisition amortises, or monetise the by-products your delivery already generates: payment history, usage data, verified identity.
  • High Replicability, low Emergence — you scale beautifully into a closing market. Launch the next wave while this one still funds you. Don't wait for the decline to show up in the numbers.
  • Both low — price and fund it as a services or lifestyle business, rather than forcing a shape onto it that it won't hold.

How to use this

Score your idea now, before you read anything else about it. Be harsh — a generous 4 buys you a plan for a business you don't have.

Your lowest lever is a roadblock standing between you and success right now — the thing quietly capping the business before it's begun. Find it, and name which of its two halves is short.

Then finish this sentence out loud: "We are a business that will spend every year buying attention." If you can unblock it, that's your first job. If you can't, decide whether you can build around it and fund the cost — while it still costs a conversation instead of a company.
