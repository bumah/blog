Most businesses don't fail because the founder is lazy or the idea is terrible. They fail because they're average at a lot of things and exceptional at nothing. I've learned this the hard way — spreading myself across ten decent features, all fine, none of them a reason for anyone to actually pay.

Because that's the thing: people don't pay for "good." They pay for one thing done noticeably better than the alternatives around them. That one thing is your reason to exist — your USP. Everything else just has to be good enough not to get in the way.

Over time I've noticed that every reason a person hands over money comes down to one of seven levers. I remember them with the word PROFITS.

P

Pleasure

Feel better, enjoy life, comfort, luxury, achievement.

R

Risk Reduction

Protect health, wealth, family, career, or future. Take away fear and uncertainty.

O

Opportunity

Access to something valuable, a chance you'd otherwise miss, a better future.

F

Financial Benefit

Make more money, save money, or protect what you already have.

I

Information

Knowledge or insight that leads to a better decision.

T

Time

Save time, get more done, automate the work, reduce the effort.

S

Social

Belong, build relationships, gain status, recognition, or identity.

You only need to win one

This is the part that took me too long to understand. You don't need to score well on all seven. You need to be undeniably the best your customer can find at one of them. A 9-out-of-10 on a single lever beats a 6-out-of-10 across all seven, every time.

Think about the businesses you pay for without hesitating. Uber won on Time — a car in three minutes. Insurance wins on Risk Reduction. Bloomberg wins on Information. Roblox wins on Pleasure — hours of fun. Rolex wins on Social — the status of the badge on your wrist. None of them are trying to be everything. They picked a lever and became the obvious choice for it.

Going through the seven

Pleasure. People pay to feel good — food, travel, luxury, entertainment, a nicer version of an ordinary thing. If your product simply makes life more enjoyable, that's a reason to pay.

Risk Reduction. Fear is one of the strongest motivators there is. Insurance, security, backups, warranties, health checks — anything that lets someone sleep easier. If you take away a worry, people will pay to keep it away.

Opportunity. We pay for doors. Courses, networks, early access, a foot in a market before everyone else. The promise is a better future than the one you'd have without it.

Financial Benefit. The most direct of all: help someone make more, save more, or lose less. If your product pays for itself, the sale almost makes itself.

Information. Knowing what others don't is worth money. Research, data, advice, a shortcut through the learning curve. People pay to make a better decision, faster.

Time. Time is the one thing nobody can make more of. Anything that gives it back — automation, delivery, done-for-you, software that turns an hour into a minute — is easy to justify paying for.

Social. We're wired for status and belonging. Communities, brands, the logo on the bag, the badge that says you're part of something. A lot of "expensive for no reason" purchases are actually people buying identity.

The mistake I kept making

For a long time I tried to be a bit of everything. A little cheaper, a little faster, a little nicer, a little more informative. It felt like covering all my bases. It was actually the surest way to be forgettable. Nobody could finish the sentence "I use it because it's the best at ___." When there's no end to that sentence, there's no business.

How to use it

Pick your lever. Look at the seven, and choose the one you can genuinely be the best at for the people you're selling to. Then pour everything into making it undeniable — so good that a customer can't find better nearby. Let the other six be "fine." They're the supporting cast, not the show.

If you can finish the sentence — "People pay us because we're the best at ___" — with one clear word from this list, you have a business. If you can't, you don't have one yet. You have a collection of average.
