A framework for optimising how long, and how well, you live.

By Terence Bumah
The Longevity Octagon™ and The Longevity Canvas™
Working paper · 2026
Summary Most longevity advice sells optimisation as something you buy: full-body scans, supplement stacks, private coaches, biohacking clinics. This paper argues the opposite. The levers that actually add healthy, secure years are cheap or free, and the barrier for most people is not money but the absence of a simple system. It also argues that longevity is more than health. Running out of money late in life ruins the back half of it, so the framework scores wealth too. The Longevity Octagon measures eight pillars across two spans, your healthspan and your wealthspan, reduces each pillar to one indicator you can track and one habit you can run, then collapses them into a single picture of where you stand and one next move, captured on the Longevity Canvas.

1. The problem

Longevity became something you buy

Most advice on living longer now points at the premium end: the €5,000 scan, the supplement stack, the clinic with a waitlist. It feels like progress, because spending is something you can control. But the spending is rarely where the years come from. The largest, best-established levers, movement, sleep, not smoking, muscle, financial security, cost almost nothing, and the exotic interventions sit at the thin, expensive margin of the evidence.

There are really two questions about your longevity. Can a person in your position add healthy, secure years at all? And what should you, specifically, do next? People pour attention into the second question, and answer it with whatever is fashionable, while skipping the boring basics that decide most of the outcome.

The failure follows a pattern. Someone optimises the measurable-but-marginal, a new supplement, and ignores the unmeasured-but-decisive, their sleep and their savings rate. Or they get their body into good shape and let one financial shock undo a decade of progress. Or they track nothing at all and simply drift. None of these is a money problem.

The Octagon is a framework for looking at your longevity from the outside, before conviction sets in. It scores eight things that decide most of the result, splits them evenly across health and wealth, and ties each one to a number you can measure without a lab and a habit you can start this week. The point is to make optimisation available to anyone, not only to people who can afford to buy it.

2. The two layers

Indicators and habits

Two things decide how long and how well you live: the numbers you are hitting, and the habits driving them. They sit in different layers, and confusing the two is where a lot of self-improvement goes wrong.

Indicators are the lag layer. They are the measurable outcomes that show how you are really doing: blood pressure, resting heart rate, grip strength, waist size, net worth, months of savings, the percentage of your income you invest. They move slowly and they are hard to argue with. Indicators tell you where you stand, and almost all of the ones that matter can be tracked with a cheap cuff, a tape measure, and a banking app.

Habits are the lead layer. They are the daily and weekly choices that shape those numbers: how you move, eat, sleep, whether you smoke, whether you save, insure, and invest. An indicator will not move because you want it to. It moves when the habits behind it change, and those habits are the only levers you actually hold.

The democratising point lives here. Both layers are cheap. What costs money is the layer people obsess over in between, the clinics and the products, and that layer is optional. Track a handful of indicators, run a handful of habits, and you have most of what the expensive version buys.

3. The eight pillars

What the Octagon measures

The framework scores eight pillars, each out of 5, four for your healthspan and four for your wealthspan. Each pillar is reduced to one indicator you can track and one keystone habit that moves it.

Each pillar carries a way to move it. Some respond to a single habit; others need you to be honest that the number is low before you will act.

Heart. Move it. Walk daily, cut the salt you do not taste, and measure your pressure so a silent problem stops being silent.

Strength. Move it. Lift something heavy twice a week. Muscle is the tissue that keeps you independent, and it responds at any age.

Endurance. Move it. Get breathless on purpose a few times a week. Aerobic capacity is one of the clearest signals of how well you are ageing.

Lifestyle. Move it. Protect your sleep first, because it is the habit that makes the others easier. Then whole food over processed, and no smoking, the single highest-value choice on this list.

Debt. Move it. Clear the highest-interest balance first and live below your means. Interest running against you does the same work as investing, in reverse.

Protection. Move it. Build a buffer of a few months before you optimise anything else, and insure only the catastrophic. This is the pillar that stops one bad event undoing years of progress.

Retirement. Move it. Automate a regular investment into something low-cost and broad, and start earlier rather than larger. Started early, the compounding does most of the work.

Income. Move it. Grow the skill that raises your rate, and add a second stream so a single setback cannot take all of it.

If you know the ULTM8 Score, this is what sits underneath it. The Octagon is the model; the Score is the reading.

4. From eight to two

Two spans, one zone

To turn eight scores into a decision, the pillars pair into two spans. The four health pillars sum into your Healthspan, out of 20. The four wealth pillars sum into your Wealthspan, out of 20. Together they are your longevity, out of 40, which the ULTM8 tool reports as a single score out of 100.

Do not average the two spans into one number and stop there. A person strong on healthspan and weak on wealthspan needs a different move from one with the reverse, and averaging hides the imbalance that tells you what to do.

Plotted against each other, the two spans place you in one of four zones:

  • Foundation. Low health, low wealth. Start anywhere; both need work.
  • Wealth Builder. Strong health, weak wealth. Fit, but one shock from trouble.
  • Health Builder. Strong wealth, weak health. Secure, but not well enough to enjoy it.
  • ULTM8. Strong on both. The corner worth aiming at, where the years are both long and good.

5. The method

Four steps

  1. Measure. Score each of the eight pillars out of 5 from your own indicators. Score cold, before you start justifying where you think you are.
  2. Collapse. Pair the pillars into Healthspan and Wealthspan, each out of 20, and place yourself in a zone.
  3. Locate. Find your weakest pillar inside your weakest span. Do not average the two; the split is what decides the move.
  4. Act. Adopt the one keystone habit for that pillar, then re-measure the indicator a season later to check it actually moved.

Where the numbers come from:

Her healthspan is ahead of her wealthspan, and the wealth weakness has a single root: money never gets set aside. Protection is a 1 and Retirement is a 2 for the same reason, so the same move should lift both.

Apply the playbook for a weak Protection and Retirement, and it points at one thing: automate the setting-aside so it stops depending on willpower. Amara sets up a single transfer on payday. 7. Scoring honestly and limitations

What the score is, and is not

Score yourself before you build the case for how you are doing. Once conviction forms, every number gets marked to the story you prefer. Score harshly. A generous score just delays the moment you find out.

Two pillars get inflated more than the rest, so anchor them. For Retirement, ask plainly what percentage of your income you actually invest: a meaningful, automated share scores 4 or 5, an untouched pension scores 2 or 3, nothing scores 1. For Protection, ask whether one bad event, a lost job, a broken boiler, a health scare, would put you into debt: if yes, it is a 1 or 2 no matter how good the rest looks.

The Octagon is a judgement tool, not a forecast, and it is general information, not medical or financial advice. The indicators are proxies; a clinician should read your health and a qualified adviser your money. What it offers is honesty. It forces the uncomfortable pillars into view and hands you one next move instead of a hundred. Treat a strong score as permission to keep going. Mostly it catches the quiet failure that was already underway before you looked.
